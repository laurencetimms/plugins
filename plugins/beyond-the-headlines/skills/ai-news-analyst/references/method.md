# The Beyond the Headlines method

This method works for any subject. The subject file (for example `subject-ai.md`) supplies the knowledge specific to one field: its typical claims, its incentives, and where verification lives.

## Seven questions for every story

1. **What kind of claim is this?**
   Separate discovery from rediscovery, a demonstration from real-world use, an announcement from evidence, a target or estimate from an outcome, and a narrow result from the general headline. Ask what was actually done, by whom, under what conditions.

2. **Who is speaking, and what do they gain?**
   Companies, investors, vendors, governments, campaigners, researchers and media outlets all have incentives. Name whose account you are relying on. An incentive doesn't make a claim false; it tells you where to look harder.

3. **What is verified, what is asserted, and what is conjecture?**
   Verified means independently checked: replication, peer review, formal proof, official data, court records. Asserted means a press release, a quote or a single outlet. Conjecture means predictions and extrapolations, including experts' ones. Label each.

4. **What are the people closest to it saying?**
   Practitioners, affected workers and domain experts, not only commentators. Note where they disagree, and with whom.

5. **What is the coverage leaving out?**
   The second-order effects, the people not quoted, the region not covered, the slower trend underneath the spike, and the earlier claim that quietly failed.

6. **Who is affected, and how?**
   Jobs and skills, how people become experts, power and who holds it, trust and institutions, wellbeing. Look for gains as seriously as harms.

7. **What would change the assessment?**
   Name the specific signals to watch: a replication, a ruling, a dataset, a deadline.

## Three things kept separate

In every item, the reader should be able to tell at a glance:

- **Reported:** what sources say happened, with links.
- **Consensus:** what people closest to it broadly agree on, or that they don't.
- **Interpretation:** your own reading, labelled "My interpretation" or similar.

## The bar for stories outside the user's interests

A brief can include up to two items outside the user's followed threads and lenses. Include one only if it meets at least three of these:

- **Structural, not episodic:** it changes rules, institutions, markets or capabilities, rather than being one-off news.
- **Wide reach:** it affects many people, a whole profession, or a whole region.
- **Independently confirmed:** more than one outlet or party, not a single company's word.
- **Under-covered:** coverage is small relative to its likely significance.
- **Durable:** likely to still matter in a year.

Say which criteria it meets. Zero items is a good answer.

## Headline or under-covered

Mark each menu item **[headline]** or **[under-covered]**. A good menu mixes both. The point is not to avoid headlines. It is to give the quieter stories equal standing.

## Tone

Plain and specific. Numbers with units, names, dates. Short sentences. No hype words, no doom words. If something is genuinely remarkable, the facts will show it.
